TITLE: Chilli Cultivation Guide

SOIL REQUIREMENTS
Chilli grows well in well-drained sandy loam to clay loam soils rich in organic matter, with a pH range of 6.0-7.0. Waterlogged conditions promote root rot and should be avoided.

NURSERY AND TRANSPLANTING
Seeds are raised in a nursery for about 30-35 days before transplanting. Healthy, disease-free seedlings should be selected, and a brief root dip in a fungicide solution before transplanting helps reduce wilt incidence.

SPACING
Common spacing is 45-60 cm between rows and 30-45 cm between plants, adjusted for variety and soil fertility.

IRRIGATION
Chilli requires regular but moderate irrigation. Flowering and fruiting stages are most sensitive to water stress. Drip irrigation with mulching is increasingly used to conserve water and reduce weed and disease pressure.

FERTILIZER MANAGEMENT
A balanced NPK program with organic manure as basal application supports healthy growth. Calcium and boron deficiencies can cause flower drop and poor fruit set, so foliar micronutrient sprays are sometimes used during flowering.

LEAF DISEASE SYMPTOMS
Leaf curl virus, spread by whiteflies, causes upward curling, crinkling, and stunted growth. Bacterial leaf spot appears as small, water-soaked spots that turn brown with a yellow halo. Anthracnose (fruit rot) shows as sunken, dark circular spots with concentric rings on ripening fruit and can also affect leaves. Cercospora leaf spot causes circular spots with a gray center and dark margin ("frog-eye" appearance).

COMMON PESTS
Thrips and mites cause leaf curling and a bronze/silvery discoloration on the underside of leaves. Fruit borer larvae bore into fruit, causing internal rot. Whitefly is a major concern both as a direct pest and as a vector for leaf curl virus.

HARVESTING
Green chillies are harvested at full size but before ripening for vegetable use, while red chillies are harvested when fully ripe and dried for spice use.

# Chilli — Crop Overview

**Crop:** Chilli / Chili Pepper
**Scientific name:** *Capsicum annuum*
**Crop category:** Vegetable / Spice crop
**Uses:** Green vegetable, dry spice, processing, pickles, sauces, and other food products.

## Note on regional variation

Recommendations for fertilizer rates, pesticide products, and application rates can vary by variety, soil type, state, season, product-registration status, and local agricultural guidance. Any answer drawing on this knowledge base should cite the source and region/date for such recommendations rather than presenting a single figure as universal.

## Production systems

Chilli is grown for two main end uses:

- **Green fruit** — harvested young for vegetable/fresh market use
- **Dry red chilli** — left to mature and ripen, then dried for spice use

It can be cultivated under both **irrigated** and **rainfed** conditions, and practices differ substantially between the two.

## Key factors affecting production

- Variety
- Soil type and drainage
- Temperature and rainfall
- Irrigation availability
- Nutrient management
- Pest and disease pressure
- Crop management practices
- Harvesting stage and timing

# Chilli — Climate Requirements

Chilli generally performs best under warm growing conditions, but several climate factors interact to determine yield and quality.

## Temperature

Temperature influences every growth stage:

- Germination
- Vegetative growth
- Flowering
- Fruit setting
- Fruit development
- Fruit colour development

**Extreme temperatures (too hot or too cold) can cause:**
- Flower drop
- Poor fruit setting
- Reduced fruit development
- General plant stress

## Rainfall

Excessive rainfall can create:
- Waterlogging
- Root problems (including root rot)
- Increased fungal and bacterial disease pressure
- Nutrient leaching/losses

Good field drainage becomes especially important during heavy rainfall periods.

## Humidity

High humidity combined with prolonged leaf wetness increases the risk of foliar and fruit diseases (see 20_anthracnose.md, 22_bacterial_leaf_spot.md, 23_powdery_mildew.md).

## Related documents

- 03_soil_requirements.md

- Poor drainage

# Chilli — Land Preparation

Land should be prepared to provide good soil tilth, adequate drainage, uniform root development, and effective weed management.

## General preparation sequence

1. Field selection
2. Removal of weeds and crop residues
3. Primary tillage
4. Soil preparation (secondary tillage/leveling)
5. Organic manure application (basal, where recommended)
6. Raised beds or ridges-and-furrows (where appropriate)
7. Planting

## Note on raised beds

In areas receiving heavy rainfall, raised beds and furrows help reduce water stagnation and root stress. ICAR advisory guidance recommends well-drained upland or medium-upland fields and raised bed-and-furrow planting where relevant to the production system.

# Chilli — Nursery Management

Chilli is commonly established through nursery-raised seedlings rather than direct seeding.

## Nursery sequence

1. Start with quality seed
2. Prepare the nursery bed
3. Treat seed (see 07_seed_treatment.md)
4. Sow
5. Establish seedlings
6. Monitor for pests/disease
7. Select healthy seedlings
8. Transplant

## Nursery requirements

- Good drainage
- Clean planting material
- Adequate sunlight
- Proper (not excessive) irrigation
- Weed management
- Regular disease monitoring

## Duration

Seeds are typically raised in the nursery for about **30–35 days** before transplanting.

## Key risk

Avoid excessive watering — prolonged moisture in the nursery increases the risk of damping-off and other seedling diseases (see 24_damping_off.md).

# Chilli — Irrigation Management

Chilli requires regular but moderate irrigation. Irrigation intervals vary by soil type and production condition — there is no single fixed schedule that applies to every farm.

## Critical growth stages for water

- Establishment
- **Flowering** (most sensitive to water stress)
- **Fruit setting** (most sensitive to water stress)
- Fruit development

Water stress during flowering and fruiting is a common cause of flower/fruit drop (see 27_flower_drop.md, 28_fruit_drop.md).

## Risks of over-irrigation

- Root problems, including root rot
- Nutrient loss/leaching
- Increased disease development
- Poor overall plant growth

## Drip irrigation

Drip irrigation, especially combined with mulching, is increasingly used because it:

- Improves water-use efficiency
- Supports fertigation
- Reduces water wastage
- Reduces weed pressure
- Reduces disease pressure from leaf wetness

## Macronutrients

- Nitrogen (N)
- Phosphorus (P)
- Potassium (K)

## Secondary and micronutrients (soil-dependent)

- Calcium
- Magnesium
- Sulphur
- Boron
- Zinc
- Iron

**Calcium and boron deficiencies** are specifically linked to flower drop and poor fruit set. Foliar micronutrient sprays during flowering are sometimes used to address this.

Nutrient management should ideally be based on:

- Soil test results
- Crop growth stage/requirement
- Variety
- Production system
- Existing soil organic matter
- Previous crop grown in the field

## Organic manure sources

- Farmyard manure
- Compost
- Vermicompost
- Suitable green manures
- Approved biofertilizers

Organic amendments improve soil structure, organic matter content, nutrient availability, and soil biological activity.

# Chilli — Leaf Curl Virus

## Causal organism / transmission

A virus (begomovirus complex) transmitted primarily by **whiteflies**.

## Symptoms

- Upward (or sometimes downward) curling of leaves
- Leaf crinkling and distortion
- Reduced leaf size
- Stunted plant growth
- Reduced fruit production

## Favourable conditions

Whitefly population buildup, particularly in warm, dry conditions, drives disease spread.

## Prevention and management

- Start with healthy, disease-free seedlings
- Protect the nursery from whitefly entry (screening/netting)
- Manage whitefly populations (see 17_whitefly.md)
- Remove severely affected plants where locally recommended, to reduce virus source
- Manage weeds that can host whiteflies or the virus
- Use resistant/tolerant varieties where available (e.g., some hybrids are bred for leaf-curl resistance)

## Related documents

- 17_whitefly.md

- White, powder-like fungal growth on leaf surfaces (often visible on the underside first)
- Leaf yellowing
- Reduced photosynthesis
- Premature leaf drop under heavy infection

## Management

Management depends on disease severity and local recommendations; general practices include improving air circulation, avoiding excess nitrogen (which promotes susceptible soft growth), and using locally approved fungicides when infection is significant.

# Chilli — Damping-Off

This disease is specifically important at the **nursery stage**.

## Symptoms

- Seedling collapse at or near the soil line
- Stem/collar damage
- Patchy, irregular seedling death in the nursery bed

## Risk factors

- Excessive nursery moisture
- Poor nursery drainage
- Overcrowded/dense sowing
- Poor nursery sanitation

## Prevention

- Ensure proper nursery drainage
- Use healthy, treated seed (see 07_seed_treatment.md)
- Water appropriately — avoid overwatering
- Use clean nursery growing media
- Avoid overcrowding seedlings

# Chilli — Root-Knot Nematode

## Symptoms

- Stunted plants
- Yellowing foliage
- Wilting, especially in warm/dry periods
- Poor nutrient and water uptake
- Characteristic root galls/knots (visible on digging up affected roots)

## Management

Integrated practices work best:

- Crop rotation with non-host crops
- Nursery sanitation
- Suitable biological control agents
- Cultural practices such as soil solarization where feasible

# Chilli — Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

Chemical control should not be the first response to a pest or disease problem. The recommended IPM sequence is:

1. Regular monitoring
2. Correct identification of the pest/disease
3. Determine severity/threshold
4. Cultural control
5. Mechanical control
6. Biological control
7. Chemical control — only when justified by severity
8. Always follow label and local recommendations for any chemical used

## IPM tools

- Sticky traps (yellow for whitefly, blue for thrips)
- Pheromone traps
- Trap crops (e.g., marigold for fruit borer)
- Field/crop sanitation
- Weed management
- Resistant/tolerant varieties
- Biological control agents
- Proper irrigation practices
- Proper plant spacing

# Chilli — Flower Drop

## Possible contributing factors

- Temperature stress (too hot or too cold)
- Water stress (both drought and waterlogging)
- Nutrient imbalance (especially calcium/boron deficiency)
- Pest damage (thrips, mites)
- Disease pressure
- Poor pollination conditions

## Diagnostic approach

Flower drop should **not** be attributed to a single cause (e.g., "nutrient deficiency") without checking context. Useful diagnostic questions:

- What is the crop age/growth stage?
- What has the recent temperature been?
- Is irrigation regular, or has there been a recent dry/wet spell?
- Are thrips or mites visible on flowers/leaves?
- Are flowers dropping before or after opening?

# Chilli — Harvesting

Harvest timing and method depend on the intended market.

## Green chilli (vegetable use)

Harvest when fruits have:

- Reached full/marketable size
- Appropriate maturity for fresh use
- Good marketable appearance

Harvested **before ripening** (i.e., while still green).

## Red / dry chilli (spice use)

Fruits are left on the plant until **fully ripe** (red), then harvested and dried for spice use.

Harvesting frequency depends on variety, local climate, and market requirements — chilli is typically harvested over multiple pickings rather than all at once.

# Chilli — Post-Harvest Management

## Sequence

1. Harvest
2. Sorting
3. Cleaning
4. Grading
5. Drying / cooling (depending on product type)
6. Packaging
7. Storage
8. Marketing

## Drying (for dry/red chilli)

Proper drying is essential for:

- Colour retention
- Overall quality
- Storage stability
- Preventing mould development

**Avoid storing inadequately dried chilli** — excess residual moisture promotes spoilage and mould growth during storage.
