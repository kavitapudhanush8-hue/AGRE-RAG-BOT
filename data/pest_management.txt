TITLE: Integrated Pest Management (IPM) Guide

PRINCIPLES OF IPM
Integrated Pest Management combines cultural, biological, mechanical, and chemical methods to keep pest populations below economically damaging levels, while minimizing environmental impact and pesticide resistance.

CULTURAL CONTROL
Crop rotation, timely sowing, field sanitation (removing crop residue and alternate hosts), and use of resistant or tolerant varieties reduce pest buildup without any direct intervention.

MONITORING AND THRESHOLDS
Regular field scouting and pheromone or light traps help detect pest presence early. Economic threshold levels (the pest density at which control action becomes cost-effective) should guide spraying decisions rather than calendar-based spraying.

BIOLOGICAL CONTROL
Natural enemies such as ladybird beetles (predators of aphids), parasitic wasps (that attack caterpillar eggs/larvae), and spiders help suppress pest populations naturally. Conserving these natural enemies by avoiding broad-spectrum insecticide overuse is a key IPM practice.

MECHANICAL CONTROL
Hand-picking of egg masses or larvae, use of sticky traps for whiteflies and thrips, and installation of bird perches to encourage natural predation are low-cost mechanical options.

CHEMICAL CONTROL
When pest levels exceed economic thresholds, selective, need-based pesticide application is preferred over routine spraying. Rotating chemical classes helps delay resistance development. Pre-harvest intervals should be respected to ensure produce safety.

COMMON CROSS-CROP PESTS
Aphids, whiteflies, and thrips are common sap-sucking pests across many crops and can also transmit viral diseases. Bollworms and fruit/pod borers affect cotton, chilli, and legumes. Termites and white grubs are soil pests that damage roots, particularly in sandy soils.
